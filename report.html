What will this flat rent for?

Weekly asking rent for all 65,777 flats in the City of Sydney, with a car space and without.

Team k-and-k · Microburbs Hackathon, 7 November 2026 · built on data dated no later than 2026-07-31

$100 vs $18
What a car space adds to the weekly rent — in a block of fewer than 20 flats, against a larger building. The same car space, six times the price.

A tenant handed an asking rent has no way to tell whether it is fair. We estimated what every flat in the council area should rent for, twice over — once with a car space and once without — and the gap between those two numbers turned out to be the interesting part.

What we found

A car space is worth six times more in a small block than in a tower

Across the whole council area the premium is about $72 a week. That average hides the real pattern. In blocks of fewer than 20 flats a space adds $100 a week. In larger buildings it adds $18.

The reason is supply inside the building, not on the street. Towers are built with basement parking, so almost every flat has a space and nobody pays extra for one. Small walk-ups and terrace conversions mostly have none, so the few spaces that exist are scarce and priced accordingly.

It matters to a tenant looking at two flats with the same asking rent, and to a landlord in a small block who is pricing a space as though it were an ordinary one.

And it varies just as much by suburb

Darlinghurst asks about $262 a week more for a flat with a space. In Chippendale the gap is $-30. Same city, same two-bedroom flat, a different answer about what a car is worth to the person renting it.

Asking rents rose about $1 a week, every week

The median advertised weekly rent went from $850 to $900 in the year to 31 July 2026, about 5.9%, or roughly $0.95 more each week.

That is why an estimate built on older adverts reads low on newer ones. We tested extrapolating the trend forward onto the scoring window and it did not improve our held-out accuracy, so we left it out rather than ship a correction we could not justify.

Each floor up is worth about $7.3 a week

Comparing flats against the median for their suburb and bedroom count, a flat on the tenth floor or above asks about $65 a week more than one on the ground, first or second. The gap builds steadily rather than jumping at any one height.

A quarter of the council area's flats have never been advertised to rent

16,094 of 65,777 flats (24.5%) have never once appeared as a rental listing. They are not spread evenly. Of 933 blocks with enough flats to measure, 117 are almost entirely rental and 16 are mostly held long term.

These are the flats an estimate has least to go on, and we say so rather than hiding them inside an average.

How we split the adverts by date

Everything dated after 2026-07-31 was dropped as the data was loaded, and the cutoff is asserted in code before any training runs — the program stops rather than continue with a later advert in the set. We then built the estimate on adverts dated before 2026-05-01 and held out 2026-05-01 to 2026-07-31 as a separate later window. Those held-out rents were never used to build or tune anything.

Two of the API's own fields would have helped and were refused: its mesh-block rent map and its suburb median rent are both dated after the cutoff, with no way to roll them back, so neither appears anywhere in this estimate.

How the estimate works

A gradient-boosted tree over the flat's suburb, bedrooms, bathrooms, floor, building size, block, position and advert history. Three choices are worth calling out.

It is fitted to the median, not the average. This task is scored on median absolute error, so the model is trained with an L1 objective, whose optimum is the conditional median — the quantity actually being measured. The usual squared-error objective targets the mean instead, which is a different number. The hyperparameter search scores candidates on median absolute error directly rather than on the training loss.

Recent adverts count for more. The advert history runs back to 2012 and rents have moved a long way since. Each advert is weighted by age with a half-life of six months.

Parking is a counterfactual, not a flag. Each flat is predicted twice, with the car space switched on and off, and the gap is held inside a band anchored on the premium measured for that flat's building size. So a tower is never given a walk-up's premium, the two columns can never contradict each other, and no flat is ever returned blank.

What it cannot see

Condition, renovation, aspect, view, internal area, and how a particular building is managed. It does not know which flats were actually advertised in August or September. Blocks and suburbs with few historic adverts fall back to coarser comparisons and carry the widest error — and roughly a quarter of flats have no advert history at all.

The typical miss is $57 a week, so this is a way to check whether an asking rent looks ordinary for its building and its street. It is not a valuation.
